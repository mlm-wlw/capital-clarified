### Section 1: The Two Factors of a Commodity — Use-Value and Value (Substance and Magnitude of Value)

#### 1. Social Wealth and the Elementary Commodity Form
In societies dominated by the capitalist mode of production, social wealth presents itself as an immense accumulation of commodities. The individual commodity stands as the elementary cellular unit of this economic order; scientific investigation therefore begins with the analytical dissection of this single cellular form.

Across diverse historical epochs—tribal communities, patriarchal peasant households, feudal manors, and communal associations—human labor produced useful goods, but social wealth never assumed the commodity-form. Under capitalism alone, products are systematically produced as commodities by independent private producers whose social metabolism is mediated through market exchange.

A commodity is an external physical object whose material properties satisfy human wants of some sort or another. The qualitative nature of these human wants—whether arising directly from the stomach or from fancy, bodily necessity or cultural desire—does not alter the nature of the thing as a use-value. Nor does it matter whether the article satisfies human wants directly as subsistence (food, clothing) or indirectly as means of production (raw materials, instruments, machinery).

Every useful object presents itself under dual aspects of quality and quantity: as an assemblage of physical properties useful in diverse ways, and as a measurable quantity. Discovering these manifold utilities and establishing standard social measures (bushels or quarters of wheat, hundredweights of iron, yards of linen, ounces of gold) is the historical work of human civilization.

#### 2. Use-Value as the Material Bearer of Wealth
The physical utility of an object constitutes its use-value. This utility is inseparable from the material properties of the commodity, possessing no existence independent of the physical body of the good itself. Wheat satisfies hunger through physical nourishment; iron serves as a plow through its tensile strength. A commodity is a use-value solely because it is a physical, material thing.

This physical utility remains completely independent of the quantity of labor required to produce or harvest it. Air, wild fruits, virgin meadows, and diamonds all possess use-value regardless of whether their acquisition demands thousands of hours of exertion or virtually no human labor. Use-value becomes realized exclusively through practical consumption or use.

Across all historical epochs, use-values constitute the material content of wealth, whatever specific social form that wealth may assume. Within capitalist society, however, use-values perform an additional, distinct function: they serve simultaneously as the physical depositories and material bearers of exchange-value.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boxed{\text{Commodity}} \implies 
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{\text{Use-Value}} & \text{Physical utility; satisfies human wants; actualized in consumption} \\[4pt]
\mathbf{\text{Exchange-Value}} & \text{Quantitative proportion in exchange; outward form of underlying Value}
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$

#### 3. Exchange-Value as the Outward Form of an Inner Substance
Exchange-value presents itself empirically as a quantitative relation or ratio in which use-values of one kind exchange against use-values of another kind. For example, 1 quarter wheat may exchange on the market for a definite quantity of shoe polish, a silk garment, or a fraction of an ounce of gold.

Because market proportions fluctuate continuously across time and space, exchange-value initially appears accidental and purely relative. From this surface volatility arose early commercial theories—advanced by Nicholas Barbon and Samuel Bailey—claiming that intrinsic value is a contradiction in terms and that value is merely the shifting ratio of an immediate transaction.

When a single commodity exchanges against diverse products in definite ratios:

$$
\begin{aligned}
1\text{ quarter wheat} = x\text{ hundredweight iron} = y\text{ silk} = z\text{ gold}
\end{aligned}
$$

each disparate commodity represents the exchange-value of 1 quarter wheat. As exchange-values, therefore, these distinct goods must be mutually replaceable and quantitatively equal. This empirical equality demonstrates two foundational principles:
1. The valid exchange-values of a given commodity express an identical social magnitude.
2. Exchange-value cannot be an autonomous essence; it is merely the necessary outward phenomenal form of appearance through which an underlying common social substance—value—manifests itself to the senses.

When two qualitatively disparate objects are equated—such as 1 quarter wheat and $x$ hundredweight of iron—the equation asserts that within two distinct physical bodies exists an identical social quantity of the same denomination, reducing both to a common third element identical in both.

$$
\begin{aligned}
1\text{ quarter wheat} = x\text{ cwt iron} &\xrightarrow{\text{Strip away physical properties}} \text{Complete abstraction from Use-Value} \\[4pt]
&\xrightarrow{\text{Strip away specific crafts}} \text{Complete abstraction from Concrete Craftsmanship} \\[4pt]
&\xrightarrow{\text{Common substance remaining}} \boxed{\mathbf{\text{Abstract Human Labor (Undifferentiated Social Labor)}}}
\end{aligned}
$$

This common denominator cannot consist of any natural, geometric, physical, or chemical property of the commodities. Physical attributes matter exclusively insofar as they endow an object with utility—making it a use-value. Yet commodity exchange is characterized precisely by total abstraction from use-value. In exchange, any one use-value is qualitatively identical to any other, provided only that it is present in the appropriate quantitative proportion. As exchange-values, commodities are mere quantities containing not an atom of physical utility.

When the physical utility of commodities is set aside, products retain only a single common property: they are all products of human labor.

#### 4. Abstract Human Labor as the Substance of Value
Abstracting from the physical use-value of commodities simultaneously abstracts from the concrete useful forms of labor that produced them. The product ceases to be the distinct output of the joiner, mason, weaver, or spinner; concrete craft forms vanish alongside physical differences.

All distinct crafts are reduced to uniform abstract human labor—the expenditure of undifferentiated human labor-power. Considered as crystals or congelations of this common substance, these objects are values. Exchange-value is not an independent property residing in physical matter, but the necessary form of appearance through which underlying social value manifests itself in market exchange.

Commodities possess value strictly insofar as they embody this common social substance. Value possesses a purely social, phantom-like objectivity containing not an atom of physical matter. Value exists neither as a physical ingredient locked inside an isolated object nor as a subjective impression in the mind of the consumer, but as an objective social reality mediated between commodities in exchange—the real abstraction of human labor within generalized commodity production.

#### 5. Measuring Value Magnitude: Socially Necessary Labor-Time
Because the substance of value is abstract human labor, the quantitative magnitude of a commodity's value is measured strictly by the duration of labor-time spent producing it, reckoned in standard temporal units such as hours, days, and weeks.

One might object that if labor duration determines value magnitude, a clumsy or lazy worker would produce an article of far greater value simply by taking more hours to complete the task.

This objection collapses because the substance of value is not arbitrary private exertion, but uniform average social labor. Society's aggregate labor-power, embodied across all commodities, counts analytically as a single homogeneous mass composed of countless individual units of labor-power.

Each individual labor-power counts as average social labor-power only insofar as it requires for production no more time than is needed on average—no more than is socially necessary:

> **Socially Necessary Labor-Time** is the labor-time required to produce any use-value under normal conditions of production, and with the average degree of skill and intensity of labor prevalent at the time.

The introduction of mechanical power-looms in England provides historical proof of this distinction. Power-looms cut by half the labor-time required to weave yarn into cloth. Hand-loom weavers continued expending twelve hours of private labor per yard; yet their twelve hours now represented only six hours of socially necessary labor, cutting their cloth's value in half. Private labor expended beyond the socially necessary average creates zero value.

Analytically, each individual commodity functions as an average sample of its class, embodying only the aliquot portion of total social labor-time required to produce commodities of that species.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boxed{\text{Magnitude of Value}} = \text{Quantity of Socially Necessary Labor-Time (SNLT)}
\end{aligned}
$$

#### 6. The Law of Labor Productivity and Value Magnitude
The magnitude of value in a commodity would remain constant if the labor-time required for its production remained fixed. However, this required duration changes continuously with every shift in the productivity of labor. The productiveness of labor is determined by five objective factors:
1. The average degree of skill and dexterity of the workers;
2. The state of science and the degree of its practical technological application;
3. The social organization of the process of production;
4. The extent and operational efficacy of the means of production (machinery and tools);
5. Physical and geographic conditions, such as soil fertility, climatic stability, and mineral richness.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boxed{\text{Unit Value}} = \frac{\text{Socially Necessary Labor-Time}}{\text{Total Physical Output}} \propto \frac{1}{\text{Productivity of Labor}}
\end{aligned}
$$

This relationship yields the fundamental law governing commodity production:

> **The Fundamental Law of Value Magnitude:** The magnitude of value of a commodity varies directly with the quantity of socially necessary labor-time embodied in it, and inversely with the productivity of the labor employed in its production.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\uparrow \text{Labor Productivity} \implies 
\begin{cases}
\uparrow \text{Physical Output (More useful articles produced per hour)} \\[4pt]
\text{Total Value Created per Hour Remains Fixed} \\[4pt]
\downarrow \text{Labor-Time Expended per Unit} \implies \boxed{\downarrow \text{Value per Single Unit}}
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$

Increased productivity yields more physical goods per hour, but total value created per hour remains constant. That constant value is distributed across more units, deflating unit value; conversely, reduced productivity increases labor-time per unit, raising individual value.

In historical reality, market prices do not mirror embodied labor-time without friction:
- Investigations by William Jacob show that gold and silver rarely sell at their full intrinsic labor-time value, because mining hazards, uncompensated labor, transport costs, and hoarding distort market realization.
- Wilhelm von Eschwege documented that the total eighty-year output of Brazilian diamond mines (1730–1810) failed to fetch the price of eighteen months of produce from Brazilian sugar and coffee plantations, despite diamonds requiring vast labor expenditures.
- Value magnitude is determined strictly by current social reproduction time rather than historical labor locked in an object. If chemical synthesis transforms carbon into diamonds with minimal labor, diamond values collapse below common building bricks, regardless of physical sparkle or hardness.

#### 7. Historical Realization and the Boundaries of Commodity Status
Commodity status is governed by four strict boundary conditions:
1. **Utility Without Value:** An article can be a use-value without possessing value whenever its physical utility is unmediated by human labor. Air, virgin soil, natural meadows, and uncultivated wild timber satisfy human wants, yet contain no expenditure of labor-power; they possess use-value but zero value.
2. **Utility and Labor Without Commodity Status:** An article can be both a use-value and the product of human labor without becoming a commodity whenever it is produced exclusively for direct personal consumption. Producing for oneself yields use-values, not commodities.
3. **Social Utility Transferred Without Market Exchange:** To become a commodity, a product must not merely be a social use-value produced for others, but must be transferred to the consumer through market exchange. A medieval peasant delivering grain as feudal tithes to the church or corvée dues to a feudal lord produced goods for others; yet these goods were not commodities because they were transferred through political coercion and custom rather than market exchange.
4. **The Absolute Boundary of Uselessness:** Nothing can have value unless it is an object of utility. If an article proves completely useless to society, the labor expended upon it is equally useless; such exertion does not count as social labor, creates zero value, and results in total realization failure.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Object possesses utility} &\xrightarrow{\text{No}} \boxed{\text{Zero Value (Wasted Labor)}} \\[4pt]
\downarrow \text{Yes (Use-Value)} & \\[4pt]
\text{Object embodies human labor} &\xrightarrow{\text{No}} \boxed{\text{Use-Value without Value (Air, virgin soil, wild timber)}} \\[4pt]
\downarrow \text{Yes} & \\[4pt]
\text{Object produced for others} &\xrightarrow{\text{No}} \boxed{\text{Use-Value for self (Personal consumption; not a commodity)}} \\[4pt]
\downarrow \text{Yes (Social Use-Value)} & \\[4pt]
\text{Mode of social transfer} &\xrightarrow{\text{Coercion / Custom}} \boxed{\text{Feudal dues/tithes (Transferred without market exchange)}} \\[4pt]
&\xrightarrow{\text{Market Exchange}} \boxed{\mathbf{\text{Commodity (Use-Value + Social Value)}}}
\end{aligned}
$$

### Section 2: The Two-Fold Character of the Labor Embodied in Commodities

#### 1. The Dual Nature of Labor in Commodity Production
Labor embodied in commodities possesses an irreducibly two-fold character: **concrete useful labor**, producing use-values, and **abstract human labor**, creating value. This dual determination is the foundational pivot upon which scientific political economy revolves; conflating these two aspects produces confusion throughout economic theory.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boxed{\text{Labor Embodied in Commodities}} \implies
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{\text{Concrete Useful Labor}} & \xrightarrow{\text{Produces}} \text{Use-Values (Physical wealth)} \\[4pt]
\mathbf{\text{Abstract Human Labor}} & \xrightarrow{\text{Creates}} \text{Value (Social substance)}
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$

#### 2. Concrete Useful Labor and the Material Metabolism of Nature
Concrete useful labor is purposeful productive activity carried out in a specific determinate form (tailoring coats, weaving linen, smelting iron, forging plows), defined by its distinct aim, specific operations, specialized instruments, and qualitative material outcome. Concrete labor determines the qualitative aspects of production: what is made, and how it is made.

Two products cannot confront each other as commodities in market exchange unless they are qualitatively different use-values resulting from qualitatively heterogeneous forms of concrete labor. Coats do not exchange against identical coats; linen does not exchange against identical linen. To the diverse varieties of use-values correspond qualitatively differentiated species of useful labor across the social division of labor.

Human labor is not the sole source of material wealth or use-values. In creating use-values, human labor acts as nature itself acts: by altering the physical forms of preexisting matter. In this transformation, human effort is constantly supported by natural forces. In the classical formulation of William Petty, labor is the father of material wealth and the earth is its mother.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boxed{\text{Human Labor (The Father)}} + \boxed{\text{Nature / Matter (The Mother)}} \xrightarrow{\text{Material Transformation}} \boxed{\text{Material Wealth (Use-Values)}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Useful labor is an eternal, nature-imposed necessity for human existence. Independent of all historically specific modes of production, useful labor remains the indispensable condition mediating the physical metabolic exchange between human beings and nature, without which human life cannot continue.

#### 3. The Social Division of Labor and Private Independent Production
The social division of labor is an indispensable prerequisite for commodity production; however, commodity production is by no means an indispensable prerequisite for the division of labor:
1. Ancient Indian village communities possessed an elaborate social division of labor without producing commodities, because production was carried out collectively for direct communal consumption.
2. Inside a modern industrial factory, labor is systematically divided into specialized operations, but this division does not proceed through workers exchanging their individual products as commodities.

Products confront each other as commodities exclusively when they are the results of different kinds of labor, each carried on independently and on the private account of isolated individuals or enterprises. Where labor is directly coordinated beforehand, products do not assume the form of commodities.

#### 4. Abstract Human Labor and Social Commensurability
When commodities are viewed strictly as values, concrete differences between craft forms disappear. Tailoring and weaving are physiologically expenditures of human brains, nerves, muscles, and sensory organs—expenditures of undifferentiated human labor-power.

While physiological expenditure occurs across all epochs, abstract human labor in the commodity-form is the objective social reduction and societal validation of this expenditure in market exchange. While concrete useful labor answers qualitative questions (*what* and *how*), abstract human labor answers quantitative questions (*how much* labor-time and *how long* that expenditure endured). As values, commodities are homogeneous crystallizations of socially validated abstract human labor.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{array}{rcc}
& \mathbf{\text{Concrete Useful Labor}} & \mathbf{\text{Abstract Human Labor}} \\[4pt]
\hline
\text{Primary Function:} & \text{Produces Use-Values} & \text{Creates Value} \\[2pt]
\text{Analytical Nature:} & \text{Qualitative (Craft techniques)} & \text{Quantitative (Labor-time)} \\[2pt]
\text{Determinations:} & \text{Qualitative (What and how)} & \text{Quantitative (Labor duration and magnitude)} \\[2pt]
\text{Historical Scope:} & \text{Universal human metabolic necessity} & \text{Specific to commodity-producing societies} \\[2pt]
\text{Productivity Impact:} & \text{Produces more physical units per hour} & \text{Value created per hour remains constant} \\[2pt]
\hline
\end{array}
\end{aligned}
$$

#### 5. The Social Reduction of Skilled Labor to Simple Average Labor
Skilled or complex labor counts analytically and practically as intensified or multiplied simple labor. A smaller quantity of complex labor equates to a greater quantity of simple, unskilled average labor:

$$
\begin{aligned}
1\text{ Hour of Skilled Labor} = k \times (1\text{ Hour of Simple Average Labor}) \quad (k > 1)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boxed{\text{Diverse Crafts (Engineer, Weaver, Mason)}} \xrightarrow{\text{Equated in Daily Market Exchange}} \boxed{\text{Definite Multiples of Simple Average Labor}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Everyday market transactions continuously execute this reduction, equating diverse crafts to definite quantities of simple average labor. The quantitative coefficients reducing complex skilled labor to simple average labor are established by an objective social process operating behind the backs of the producers, codified through habit, custom, and continuous market exchange.

*Methodological Scope:* The analysis brackets wages and worker compensation: whether a laborer receives a wage, or what proportion of value that wage represents, is irrelevant to determining value magnitude by total embodied labor-time.

#### 6. The Inverse Movement Between Material Wealth and Value Magnitude
An increase in use-values constitutes an expansion of material wealth: two coats clothe two people, whereas one coat clothes only one. Yet increased material wealth may correspond to a simultaneous, sharp decline in total value.

This divergent movement arises directly from the two-fold character of labor:
- Productive efficiency pertains exclusively to labor in its concrete useful form. A rise in productivity increases the quantity of physical goods created in an hour.
- Productive efficiency has zero bearing on labor considered as abstract human labor. Equal periods of labor-time of average intensity always create identical amounts of value, regardless of changes in productivity.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{array}{ccc}
\boxed{\uparrow \text{Labor Productivity}} & \implies & \boxed{\uparrow \text{Material Wealth (More physical coats produced)}} \\[4pt]
& & \Downarrow \\[4pt]
\boxed{\text{Total Value per Hour is Fixed}} & \implies & \boxed{\downarrow \text{Value per Single Coat (Less SNLT per item)}}
\end{array}
\end{aligned}
$$

The same rise in labor productivity that expands material abundance simultaneously deflates the unit value of each individual item, while leaving the total value yielded by an hour of labor constant. Technological progress routinely expands material abundance while lowering unit values.

### Section 3: The Form of Value or Exchange-Value

#### 1. The Necessity of the Value-Form
Commodities are dual entities: both objects of physical utility and bearers of value. Consequently, they possess a two-fold form: a natural bodily form (as use-values) and a value-form (as exchange-values).

Value possesses a purely social, supersensible reality containing not an atom of physical matter. An isolated commodity cannot reveal value in its own natural body or physical form, because commodities acquire value objectivity solely as crystallizations of a common social substance—abstract human labor.

> **The Principle of Value Expression:** Value can manifest itself only in the social relation where one commodity confronts another.

Bourgeois political economy failed to discover the genesis of the money-form because it never analyzed the development of the value expression from its embryonic germ through to its monetary manifestation. To dissolve the enigma of money, the value-form develops through four stages:
1. **Form A (The Elementary Form of Value):** A single commodity expresses its value in one other commodity.
2. **Form B (The Expanded Form of Value):** A commodity expresses its value across an open-ended series of other goods.
3. **Form C (The General Form of Value):** All commodities express their values in a single shared equivalent.
4. **Form D (The Money-Form):** The role of universal equivalent becomes permanently monopolized by gold.

#### 2. The Elementary Form of Value (Form A)
The simplest expression of value occurs when two individual commodities are equated: 20 yards of linen = 1 coat.

$$
\begin{aligned}
20\text{ yards of linen} = 1\text{ coat}
\end{aligned}
$$

This elementary form of value contains within itself the entire secret of all higher forms of value. In this equation, the two commodities occupy opposing, mutually dependent, and strictly asymmetrical poles:
- **The Relative Form of Value (The Active Pole):** The linen plays the active role; it seeks expression and expresses its value in the coat.
- **The Equivalent Form (The Passive Pole):** The coat serves as the physical material in which the linen's value is expressed; it plays the passive role, functioning as the tangible embodiment of value.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{array}{ccc}
\boxed{\mathbf{20\text{ Yards Linen}}} & \overset{\text{expresses value in}}{\xrightarrow{\hspace{3cm}}} & \boxed{\mathbf{1\text{ Coat}}} \\[6pt]
\left[\begin{array}{c} \mathbf{\text{Relative Value-Form}} \\[2pt] \text{Active pole; seeks expression} \\[2pt] \text{Value magnitude represented} \\[2pt] \text{Cannot express value in itself} \end{array}\right] & & \left[\begin{array}{c} \mathbf{\text{Equivalent Value-Form}} \\[2pt] \text{Passive pole; bodily mirror} \\[2pt] \text{Directly exchangeable} \\[2pt] \text{Concrete body represents value} \end{array}\right]
\end{array}
\end{aligned}
$$

The relative and equivalent forms are inseparable yet mutually exclusive extremes:
1. A commodity cannot occupy both positions simultaneously in the same expression. Linen cannot express its value in itself: $20\text{ yards linen} = 20\text{ yards linen}$ is a tautology expressing only physical self-identity, not value.
2. To express value relatively, Commodity A requires a distinct Commodity B confronting it as equivalent.
3. The commodity serving as equivalent cannot simultaneously assume the relative form; its role is solely to serve as the physical embodiment of A's value.
4. Inverting the equation ($1\text{ coat} = 20\text{ yards linen}$) reverses the polar functions: the coat becomes relative, and linen becomes the equivalent.

Human labor-power in motion (the active process of laboring) creates value, but is not itself value. Labor becomes value only when it becomes congealed, static, and objectified in a commodity body within a social relation of exchange. Because a commodity cannot express its value in its own physical body, it must undergo an objective bifurcation, doubling itself into its natural bodily form and an external value-form, projecting its internal contradiction onto the external world of goods.

#### 3. Polar Asymmetry and the Relational Value Mirror
In the category of commodities, the value expression can be modeled as a directed morphism $v: A \to B$, where Commodity $A$ is the domain (the relative form) and Commodity $B$ is the codomain (the equivalent form). 

While the identity morphism on utility preserves physical self-identity, the value morphism is strictly non-reflexive:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Identity Morphism on Utility:} \quad & \text{id}_A: A \xrightarrow{\text{use-value}} A \quad (20\text{ yards linen} = 20\text{ yards linen}) \\[6pt]
\text{Value Morphism Non-Reflexivity:} \quad & v: A \xrightarrow{\text{value}} B \quad (B \neq A)
\end{aligned}
$$

In the relative form, Commodity A endows its value with objective existence by equating the bodily substance of Commodity B to value itself, using B's physical use-value as a mirror to reflect A's own social value. In equating linen to the coat, the body of the coat embodies value: concrete tailoring is equated to concrete weaving, reducing tailoring to what is common to both—abstract human labor.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{array}{ccc}
\boxed{\text{Commodity } A \text{ (Domain)}} & \xrightarrow{\quad v \quad} & \boxed{\text{Commodity } B \text{ (Codomain)}} \\[4pt]
\left[\begin{array}{c} \mathbf{\text{Relative Form}} \\[2pt] \text{Active pole; seeks expression} \\[2pt] \text{Value magnitude represented} \\[2pt] \text{Cannot express value in itself} \end{array}\right] & & \left[\begin{array}{c} \mathbf{\text{Equivalent Form}} \\[2pt] \text{Passive pole; bodily mirror} \\[2pt] \text{Directly exchangeable} \\[2pt] \text{Concrete body represents value} \end{array}\right]
\end{array}
\end{aligned}
$$

#### 4. Quantitative Variations in Relative Value
The equation $20\text{ yards linen} = 1\text{ coat}$ implies that both contain equal socially necessary labor-time. Shifts in labor productivity produce four distinct quantitative permutations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{array}{lcccc}
\hline
\textbf{Permutation} & \textbf{Linen Labor-Time} & \textbf{Coat Labor-Time} & \textbf{Relative Expression} & \textbf{Outcome} \\[2pt]
\hline
\text{Case I (Direct)} & \text{Doubles / Halves} & \text{Constant} & 20\text{ yds linen} = \mathbf{2\text{ coats} / \frac{1}{2}\text{ coat}} & \text{Linen relative value doubles / halves} \\[2pt]
\text{Case II (Inverse)} & \text{Constant} & \text{Doubles / Halves} & 20\text{ yds linen} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{2}\text{ coat} / 2\text{ coats}} & \text{Linen relative value halves / doubles} \\[2pt]
\text{Case III (Static)} & \text{Changes by } r & \text{Changes by } r & 20\text{ yds linen} = \mathbf{1\text{ coat}} & \text{Ratio static despite real value shifts} \\[2pt]
\text{Case IV (Compound)} & \text{Unequal shift} & \text{Unequal shift} & \text{Compound divergence} & \text{Complex relational shift} \\[2pt]
\hline
\end{array}
\end{aligned}
$$

In Case I, when linen value changes while coat value remains constant, linen's relative value varies directly with its own value. In Case II, when linen value remains constant while coat value changes, linen's relative value varies inversely with the equivalent's value. In Case III, when both values change simultaneously in equal proportions and directions, the relative ratio remains static. In Case IV, opposite or unequal variations produce compound combinations of these movements.

These permutations establish an essential principle: **real changes in the magnitude of value are neither unequivocally nor exhaustively reflected in relative market expressions.** A commodity's relative value may alter while its real value remains constant, its relative value may remain constant while its real value alters, and simultaneous shifts in value and relative expression need not correspond in amount.

Critics (such as Broadhurst) seized upon this discrepancy to deny the labor theory of value, arguing that if Commodity A changes its relative value merely because Commodity B altered in value without any change in labor on A, labor cannot regulate value.

This objection commits an elementary mathematical error: confusing the numerator of a fraction with the fractional ratio itself:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\text{Value of Linen}}{\text{Value of Coat}} = \frac{10}{20} = \frac{1}{2} \quad \xrightarrow{\text{Coat value rises to } 50} \quad \frac{10}{50} = \frac{1}{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boxed{\text{Numerator (Linen) remains fixed at } 10} \quad \text{while the relational ratio drops from } \frac{1}{2} \text{ to } \frac{1}{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

A ratio alters whenever its denominator changes, leaving the numerator unchanged. Confusing a shifting relational proportion with the absolute magnitude of the numerator is an elementary mathematical error.

#### 5. The Three Peculiarities of the Equivalent Form
In the equivalent form, three structural inversions occur:
1. **The First Peculiarity:** Physical use-value becomes the form of appearance of its opposite, value. The raw physical body of the coat serves directly as the visible manifestation of the linen's non-physical social value.
2. **The Second Peculiarity:** Concrete useful labor becomes the form of manifestation of its opposite, abstract human labor. The specific craft labor of tailoring counts solely as the tangible incarnation of undifferentiated human labor.
3. **The Third Peculiarity:** Private labor carried on independently takes the form of its opposite, labor in directly social form. The isolated private labor producing the equivalent commodity is treated immediately as labor recognized by and performed for society as a whole.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{In the Equivalent Form (The Coat):} \implies
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{\text{Use-Value}} &\xrightarrow{\text{serves as the mirror of}} \mathbf{\text{Value}} \\[4pt]
\mathbf{\text{Concrete Labor (Tailoring)}} &\xrightarrow{\text{serves as the embodiment of}} \mathbf{\text{Abstract Human Labor}} \\[4pt]
\mathbf{\text{Private Independent Labor}} &\xrightarrow{\text{takes the form of}} \mathbf{\text{Directly Social Labor}}
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$

*Relational Boundary:* These three peculiarities hold for the coat exclusively within and during the specific value relation in which it stands as equivalent to the linen. Outside this relation, the coat reverts to being an ordinary commodity whose labor is private, concrete, and unvalidated.

#### 6. The Classical Limit: Aristotle on Commensurability and Social Equality
Aristotle was the first thinker to analyze the form of value, discerning in the equation 5 beds = 1 house ($5\text{ beds} = 1\text{ house}$) that exchange cannot take place without equality, and equality cannot exist without commensurability.

However, Aristotle halted his analysis in philosophical perplexity, concluding that it is in reality impossible for qualitatively distinct objects to be truly commensurable, and asserting that exchange was merely an artificial social expedient for practical convenience.

The historical barrier that blocked Aristotle was the structure of Greek society, which was founded upon slavery and took the natural inequality of human beings and their labor-powers as an unquestioned social axiom. A slave-owning society could not conceive that diverse human crafts are equal as abstract human labor.

The secret of the value expression—that all labors are equal because they are expenditures of identical human labor—can be deciphered only when the concept of human equality has acquired the permanent fixity of a popular prejudice. This is possible exclusively in a society where generalized commodity production prevails, and where human beings relate to one another primarily as commodity owners.

#### 7. The Expanded Form of Value (Form B) and Its Structural Limits
The elementary form is an accidental germ expressing value in terms of a single arbitrary commodity, leaving value dependent on a contingent, private relationship. To overcome this limitation, the value relation expands into the Total or Expanded Form of Value:

$$
\begin{aligned}
20\text{ yards linen} = 1\text{ coat} \quad \text{or} \quad = 10\text{ lbs tea} \quad \text{or} \quad = 1\text{ quarter corn} \quad \text{or} \quad = 2\text{ oz gold} \dots
\end{aligned}
$$

Category-theoretically, Form B represents a single source object projecting an infinite family of divergent morphisms into the commodity realm:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{array}{rcl}
& \xrightarrow{\quad e_1 \quad} & \boxed{\text{Commodity } B \text{ (1 coat)}} \\[4pt]
& \xrightarrow{\quad e_2 \quad} & \boxed{\text{Commodity } C \text{ (10 lbs tea)}} \\[4pt]
\boxed{\text{Commodity } A \text{ (20 yds linen)}} & \xrightarrow{\quad e_3 \quad} & \boxed{\text{Commodity } D \text{ (1 qr corn)}} \\[4pt]
& \xrightarrow{\quad e_4 \quad} & \boxed{\text{Commodity } E \text{ (2 oz gold)}} \\[4pt]
& \xrightarrow{\quad \dots \quad} & \dots
\end{array}
\end{aligned}
$$

The dialectical advance of the Expanded Form consists in proving that value is not an accidental ratio between two objects, but an objective crystallization of undifferentiated human labor that systematically regulates exchange across the entire realm of commodities.

However, the Expanded Form suffers from three fatal dialectical defects:
1. **Interminability:** The chain is endless and incomplete, constantly extending whenever a new commodity is produced.
2. **Disjointed Mosaic:** It presents a fragmented patchwork of independent expressions lacking social unity.
3. **Fragmentation:** Each commodity constructs its own distinct infinite series, producing mutually incompatible standards of equivalence.

#### 8. The General Form of Value (Form C) as Universal Social Integration
A categorical breakthrough occurs through universal diagrammatic inversion: when the expanded series is reversed, all commodities express their values in a single, common, socially excluded commodity:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left.
\begin{array}{r}
1\text{ coat} \\[2pt]
10\text{ lbs tea} \\[2pt]
1\text{ quarter corn} \\[2pt]
2\text{ oz gold} \\[2pt]
x\text{ Commodity } A_n
\end{array}
\right\} = \boxed{\mathbf{20\text{ yards of linen}}} \quad (\text{Universal Equivalent } U)
\end{aligned}
$$

All commodities now express their value with simplicity and social unity. The excluded commodity (linen) becomes the **Universal Equivalent**, serving as the universal target object or colimit in the category of commodities:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{array}{ccc}
\boxed{\text{Commodity } A_1 \text{ (1 coat)}} & \xrightarrow{\quad \mu_1 \quad} & \\[4pt]
\boxed{\text{Commodity } A_2 \text{ (10 lbs tea)}} & \xrightarrow{\quad \mu_2 \quad} & \boxed{\mathbf{U} \text{ (20 yards linen)}} \\[4pt]
\boxed{\text{Commodity } A_3 \text{ (1 qr corn)}} & \xrightarrow{\quad \mu_3 \quad} & \left[\begin{array}{c} \mathbf{\text{Universal Target Object / Colimit}} \\[2pt] \text{All private labors factor through } U \\[2pt] \text{Direct universal exchangeability} \end{array}\right] \\[4pt]
\boxed{\text{Commodity } A_n \text{ (Commodity } n\text{)}} & \xrightarrow{\quad \mu_n \quad} &
\end{array}
\end{aligned}
$$

Every pairwise exchange between two commodities $A_i$ and $A_j$ factors uniquely through the universal equivalent object $U$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{array}{ccc}
\boxed{\text{Commodity } A_i} & \xrightarrow{\quad f_{ij} \quad} & \boxed{\text{Commodity } A_j} \\[4pt]
& \searrow{\mu_i} \quad \nearrow{\mu_j^{-1}} & \\[4pt]
& \boxed{\mathbf{U}} &
\end{array}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $f_{ij} = \mu_j^{-1} \circ \mu_i$. Every disparate private labor embodied in $A_i$ is mapped into $U$, where it is transformed into directly social abstract labor.

The General Form of Value results from the collective social action of the entire commodity world: a commodity attains the status of universal equivalent only because all other commodities collectively isolate it as their common mirror.

This establishes the principle of polar exclusion:
- A single commodity acquires direct universal exchangeability only because all other commodities are denied that property.
- The universal equivalent is strictly excluded from having a general relative form within that system; money cannot express its own value in itself ($20\text{ yards linen} = 20\text{ yards linen}$ is a degenerate identity). To express its own relative value, the universal equivalent must be equated back into the expanded series (Form B).

This structural exclusion refutes utopian proposals (such as Pierre-Joseph Proudhon's and Robert Owen's) that envision retaining commodity production while abolishing money through directly exchangeable labor-money. Direct universal exchangeability requires a single excluded pole; universalizing it destroys the polar condition required for exchange, as absurd as imagining that all Catholics could simultaneously be popes.

#### 9. The Money-Form (Form D) and the Genesis of Price
Between the General Form (Form C) and the Money-Form (Form D), no structural difference exists, save that social custom has definitively monopolized the universal equivalent role in the physical substance of gold:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left.
\begin{array}{r}
20\text{ yards linen} \\[2pt]
1\text{ coat} \\[2pt]
10\text{ lbs tea} \\[2pt]
1\text{ quarter corn}
\end{array}
\right\} = \boxed{\mathbf{2\text{ ounces of gold}}} \implies \boxed{\text{Price: } \$40}
\end{aligned}
$$

Category-theoretically, every commodity $A_i$ now admits a unique canonical price morphism into the physical gold commodity $G$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{array}{ccc}
\boxed{\text{Commodity } A_i} & \xrightarrow{\quad p_i \quad} & \boxed{\mathbf{G} \text{ (Gold Commodity)}} \\[4pt]
\left[\begin{array}{c} \text{Relative Commodity} \\[2pt] \text{Private, concrete labor} \end{array}\right] & & \left[\begin{array}{c} \mathbf{\text{Universal Money Object}} \\[2pt] \text{Directly social equivalent} \\[2pt] \text{Universal purchasing power} \end{array}\right]
\end{array}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{array}{clc}
\mathbf{\text{Form A (Elementary):}} & 20\text{ yards linen} = 1\text{ coat} & [\text{Accidental, one-on-one trade}] \\[4pt]
& \Downarrow & \\[4pt]
\mathbf{\text{Form B (Expanded):}} & 20\text{ yards linen} = \begin{cases} 1\text{ coat} \\ 10\text{ lbs tea} \\ 1\text{ quarter corn} \\ 2\text{ oz gold} \end{cases} & [\text{Endless, disconnected chain}] \\[4pt]
& \Downarrow \text{ (Universal diagrammatic inversion)} & \\[4pt]
\mathbf{\text{Form C (General):}} & \left. \begin{array}{l} 1\text{ coat} \\ 10\text{ lbs tea} \\ 1\text{ quarter corn} \end{array} \right\} = \boxed{20\text{ yards linen}} & \left[\begin{array}{c} \text{Single universal equivalent} \\ \text{Unified social colimit} \end{array}\right] \\[4pt]
& \Downarrow \text{ (Social monopoly fixed on gold)} & \\[4pt]
\mathbf{\text{Form D (Money):}} & \left. \begin{array}{l} 20\text{ yards linen} \\ 1\text{ coat} \\ 10\text{ lbs tea} \end{array} \right\} = \boxed{2\text{ ounces gold}} & \left[\begin{array}{c} \text{Universal equivalent is gold} \\ \text{Price: expressed in money} \end{array}\right]
\end{array}
\end{aligned}
$$

The commodity nature of money is fundamental: gold functions as money only because it was previously an ordinary commodity possessing value as a product of human labor. Money is neither an arbitrary legal convention nor a mere symbol, but an actual commodity whose physical body has become socially fused with the universal equivalent role.

The Price-Form is simply the elementary relative value-form of a commodity expressed in terms of the money commodity ($20\text{ yards linen} = 2\text{ ounces gold}$, representing \$40). The mystery of money is solved: it is simply the universal equivalent form, whose entire enigma was present in embryo within the elementary exchange equation ($20\text{ yards linen} = 1\text{ coat}$).

### Section 4: The Fetishism of Commodities and the Secret Thereof

#### 1. The Mystical Character of the Commodity-Form
A commodity appears at first glance to be a trivial, readily understood object; yet rigorous scientific analysis reveals it to be a strange thing, full of metaphysical subtleties and theological niceties.

The origin of this mystical character does **not** arise from:
1. **Not from physical use-value:** Whether natural timber is fashioned into a table or iron into a plow, physical goods satisfy tangible human wants through their physical properties.
2. **Not from the physiological nature of labor:** Expending human brains, nerves, muscles, and sensory organs is a universal biological condition of human life across all eras.
3. **Not from the temporal duration of labor:** In all historical epochs, tracking the labor-time required to secure subsistence has interested human beings.
4. **Not from the fact that labor is socially interdependent:** People have worked in social interdependence across history.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{array}{lcc}
\hline
\textbf{Element} & \textbf{Material Characteristics} & \textbf{Explains Commodity Fetish} \\[2pt]
\hline
\text{Physical Use-Value} & \text{Tangible material utility} & \textbf{No} \\[2pt]
\text{Physiology of Labor} & \text{Universal expenditure of human energy} & \textbf{No} \\[2pt]
\text{Duration of Labor-Time} & \text{Measuring temporal duration} & \textbf{No} \\[2pt]
\text{Social Interdependence} & \text{Human cooperation across epochs} & \textbf{No} \\[2pt]
\mathbf{\text{The Commodity-Form}} & \mathbf{\text{Private labor validated only in exchange}} & \mathbf{\text{YES: Sole Source of Mystery}} \\[2pt]
\hline
\end{array}
\end{aligned}
$$

The mystical character of commodities arises strictly and exclusively from the commodity-form itself:
- The social equality of human labor assumes the objective form of the equal market value of products;
- Measuring labor expenditure by time assumes the form of the quantitative magnitude of value;
- The mutual social relations of the producers assume the fantastic form of a social relation between the physical products of their labor.

#### 2. The Social Inversion: Relations Between People Disguised as Relations Between Things
In commodity production, the social characteristics of men's labor are reflected back to them as objective properties stamped upon the products of labor themselves. The relation of producers to society's aggregate labor presents itself not as a direct social relation between persons in their work, but as a material relation between persons and a social relation between things.

This objective substitutive inversion (*quid pro quo* [substituting one thing for another]) forms the core of reification. In category-theoretic terms, the category of human productive relations and the category of physical market transactions form a reification commuting square:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{array}{ccc}
\boxed{\begin{array}{c} \text{Social Relations Between Persons} \\[2pt] \text{(Interdependent Private Labors } L_i, L_j\text{)} \end{array}} & \xrightarrow{\quad \text{Social Division of Labor} \quad} & \boxed{\begin{array}{c} \text{Material Relations Between Persons} \\[2pt] \text{(Monetary Wages, Contracts, Sales)} \end{array}} \\[10pt]
\Big\downarrow \scriptstyle{\text{Real Abstraction (Reification)}} & & \Big\downarrow \scriptstyle{\text{Market Domination}} \\[10pt]
\boxed{\begin{array}{c} \text{Physical Bodies of Useful Goods} \\[2pt] \text{(Use-Values } C_i, C_j\text{)} \end{array}} & \xrightarrow[\quad \text{Commuting Exchange Morphism } f_{ij} \quad]{} & \boxed{\begin{array}{c} \text{Social Relations Between Things} \\[2pt] \text{(Commodity Values \& Price Movements)} \end{array}}
\end{array}
\end{aligned}
$$

In physical vision, in the optics of the eye, light rays physically travel from an external object to the optic nerve—an actual physical transmission between physical things. In contrast, commodity fetishism involves zero physical transmission between objects. It is purely a definite social relationship between human beings that assumes, in their eyes, the fantastic form of a relation between things.

The true analogy exists in religion: creations of the human brain appear as autonomous beings endowed with independent life, entering into relations with each other and with humanity. Exactly so in the world of commodities: products of human hands become autonomous commodities that interact on markets and rule over their human creators.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{array}{ccc}
\mathbf{\text{Actual Underlying Reality}} & & \mathbf{\text{Inverted Market Appearance}} \\[2pt]
\hline
\text{Equality of human labor-power} & \implies & \text{Equal exchange-value of physical products} \\[2pt]
\text{Duration of social labor-time} & \implies & \text{Quantitative market price of goods} \\[2pt]
\text{Mutual relations between producers} & \implies & \text{Autonomous relations between commodities} \\[2pt]
\hline
\end{array}
\end{aligned}
$$

#### 3. The Material Basis of Social Illusion in Private Production
The social groundwork of commodity fetishism is the regime of private, independent producers who carry on their productive activities in isolation. The sum total of these private labors constitutes society's aggregate labor; but because producers do not enter into social contact until they exchange their products on the market, the social character of each individual's labor asserts itself exclusively *post festum* [after the fact], in market exchange.

Consequently:
1. Labor must satisfy a definite social want as concrete useful labor;
2. Labor can satisfy the producer's own manifold needs only insofar as the mutual exchangeability of private labors is established in the market, reducing distinct crafts to abstract human labor.

When commodity producers equate different products in exchange as values, they do not consciously recognize them as embodiments of abstract human labor. Rather, equating products as values in exchange objectively and unconsciously equates disparate labors as human labor in practice: they do not know it, but they do it. Value converts every product of labor into a social hieroglyphic that society later attempts to decipher.

To the isolated producer, exchange proportions appear to reside in the physical nature of the products themselves. Once exchange ratios achieve customary stability, quantities of value fluctuate continuously, independent of the foresight or conscious intent of producers. Their own social movement takes the form of the movement of autonomous things, controlling the producers instead of being controlled by them.

In an unregulated commodity economy, socially necessary labor-time forcibly asserts itself behind the backs of producers like a blind law of nature—analogous to the law of gravity asserting itself when a house collapses about one's ears.

#### 4. The Persistence of Reification Beyond Scientific Discovery
The scientific discovery that commodities are products of socially necessary human labor marks a decisive theoretical breakthrough, but it by no means dissipates the objective reality of commodity fetishism in daily practice.

Just as discovering the chemical composition of the atmosphere leaves the physical perception of air as gas unaltered, the scientific deciphering of value leaves everyday commercial reality intact. To commodity producers, value remains an immutable natural fact, because the material groundwork—isolated private production—continues in daily life. Fetishism is an objective social illusion rooted in practical reality, rather than a subjective intellectual error that can be dispelled by education alone.

#### 5. Transparent Modes of Production
To demonstrate that commodity fetishism is not an eternal law of nature but the historical result of a specific mode of production, four alternative systems exhibit completely transparent production:

1. **An Isolated Individual Living Alone on an Island:**
   An isolated individual living alone on an island must apportion labor-time among toolmaking, shelter building, hunting, and farming. Necessity compels this individual to divide available hours among distinct tasks. All relations between labor and self-created wealth are simple, direct, and transparent. All essential determinations of value (labor-time, physical utility) are present, without assuming any mysterious commodity-form.

2. **The European Middle Ages:**
   In feudal Europe, social relations are founded upon direct personal dependence: serfs, lords, and clergy confront each other in personal subordination. Because personal dependence constitutes the groundwork of society, labor and its products need not assume a fantastic form different from their reality. Services and payments in kind (such as compulsory corvée field labor and grain dues) represent labor directly in its social form. The serf knows with precision that what is rendered to the lord is a definite quantity of personal labor. Social relations between individuals in work appear as their mutual personal relations, not disguised as relations between things.

3. **The Traditional Peasant Household:**
   Within a traditional peasant family producing grain, livestock, yarn, and clothing for domestic use, goods are shared products of familial labor. Distinct tasks (tillage, spinning, weaving) are directly social functions of the family unit, distributed by age, sex, and seasonal conditions. Individual labor-power operates transparently as an aliquot portion of total family labor, without products becoming commodities traded between family members.

4. **A Planned Association of Free Producers:**
   In an association of free human beings working with shared means of production under a conscious social plan, all characteristics of transparent labor are reproduced on a societal scale:
   - The aggregate product is a social product: one portion serves as fresh means of production and remains collective; another portion is distributed for individual consumption.
   - Where each worker's share of subsistence is determined by labor-time, labor-time performs a double, transparent function:
     1. Planned social apportionment maintains the proportion between distinct kinds of work and societal needs;
     2. It measures the individual producer's participation in common labor and personal share of consumable produce.
   Social relations of producers, with regard both to their labor and to their products, remain completely transparent in both production and distribution.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{array}{lcccc}
\hline
\textbf{Economic System} & \textbf{Organization of Labor} & \textbf{Contact Between Producers} & \textbf{Fetishism Status} \\[2pt]
\hline
\text{Isolated Individual on Island} & \text{Individual self-direction} & \text{Directly transparent} & \text{None (Relations are obvious)} \\[2pt]
\text{Feudal Middle Ages} & \text{Direct personal dependence} & \text{Direct labor services in kind} & \text{None (Personal dues are transparent)} \\[2pt]
\text{Traditional Peasant Family} & \text{Domestic familial cooperation} & \text{Direct shared labor} & \text{None (Work distributed by custom)} \\[2pt]
\mathbf{\text{Capitalist Commodity Production}} & \mathbf{\text{Isolated private producers}} & \mathbf{\text{Indirectly via market exchange}} & \mathbf{\text{Full Fetishism (Things rule people)}} \\[2pt]
\text{Planned Free Association} & \text{Conscious collective plan} & \text{Direct planned cooperation} & \text{Dissolved (Planned and transparent)} \\[2pt]
\hline
\end{array}
\end{aligned}
$$

The mystical veil shrouding society's material life-process will not be stripped away until production becomes the production of freely associated human beings, consciously regulated in accordance with a settled social plan. This conscious regulation demands a material foundation that can itself be only the historical result of a long and arduous development.

#### 6. The Historical Blind Spots of Classical Economic Theory
Classical political economy (culminating in Adam Smith and David Ricardo) achieved significant scientific advances by analyzing value and discovering that labor-time underlies exchange-value. However, classical economics suffered from decisive structural blind spots:
1. It never inquired why labor assumes the form of value, and why labor-time assumes the magnitude of value.
2. It failed to distinguish clearly between concrete useful labor (producing use-values) and abstract human labor (creating value).
3. Ricardo treated the value-form as a matter of indifference, viewing it as having no organic connection to the nature of commodities, thereby conflating the historical social form of labor with its material content.

Traditional economic analysis fell into the **retrospective trap**: human reflection follows a course opposite to actual historical development. Scientific inquiry begins retrospectively, looking backward after historical events have already crystallized (*post festum* [after the fact]), taking finished results (money, prices, commercial markets) as ready-made starting points.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boxed{\text{Historical Development of Exchange}} \xrightarrow{\text{Produces finished result}} \boxed{\text{Prices, Markets, and Money}} \\[6pt]
\Downarrow \text{ Retrospective reflection starts backward from finished results} \\[6pt]
\boxed{\text{Finished Market Forms}} \xrightarrow{\text{Naturalized into}} \boxed{\mathbf{\text{Eternal Laws of Nature}}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Because the commodity-form and money acquired the stability of natural social forms before theoretical reflection began, political economy took these historically emerged forms for granted as eternal natural laws.

Economists treated bourgeois institutions as eternal natural laws and feudal institutions as artificial creations. This attitude directly parallels the Church Fathers who regarded pagan religions as human inventions while viewing their own religion as divine revelation: economists acknowledged that there has been history, but asserted that there is no longer any history ("there has been history, but now there is no longer any").

Vulgar political economy abandoned scientific inquiry altogether, confining itself to organizing the everyday prejudices of commercial practice. Vulgar economics fell into the superstition of regarding value as an inherent physical attribute of things, imagining that soil naturally produces rent and capital produces profit as physical emanations, completely blind to the human social relations that alone constitute value.
